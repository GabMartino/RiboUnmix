Why a small set of synthetic gamma sites is still missed

A forensic report connecting the gamma calibration plume, the matched alpha-causality ablation, the concentration of errors at transcript boundaries, and the discovery that the model receives CDS sequence but not the UTR nucleotides required to complete terminal 30-nt RPF windows.

Short answer

Gamma recovery is globally excellent, but a small and highly reproducible set of strong programmed sites is predicted close to neutral. Learned dispersion is elevated at these sites, yet the matched interventions show that alpha is mostly a marker of the failure rather than its cause. The decisive observation is spatial: 98–99% of the strong misses occur within three codons of a CDS edge. At those positions, the simulator's ideal RPF rule uses 5′- or 3′-UTR nucleotides that are absent from the current CDS-only model input.

Main conclusion. The current model appears capable of learning the programmed sequence rules when their complete sequence window is observable. Exact terminal recovery requires supplying raw transcript flanks—not handcrafted bias labels and not another alpha correction.

Most learned-vs-programmed points lie close to the identity line. The conspicuous exception is a thin group with high programmed log-gamma but learned log-gamma near zero. These are catastrophic local misses even though the pooled gamma correlation remains above 0.99.

2. What is classified as a strong gamma miss?

In the saved analysis, g denotes gauge-fixed log-gamma. A site is strong when g_true ≥ 1, and it is counted as missed when g_learned < 0.5. The reported rate is therefore:

P(g_learned < 0.5 | g_true ≥ 1).

This threshold deliberately isolates the visually obvious plume. It is not a replacement for pooled PCC, RMSE, calibration slope, or continuous error; it answers a narrower question: how often does the model nearly neutralize a strongly programmed bias?

3. The causal question: can learned dispersion hide gamma?

The synthetic counts were generated with a known NB2 dispersion, \(\alpha_{\mathrm{true}}=0.1\). The model nevertheless learns a position-specific \(\alpha_{dti}\). This is useful for a realistic experiment, but it creates a possible failure mechanism: the same dispersion that describes noisy counts also changes how strongly the likelihood trains the predicted mean \(\mu\), and therefore the shared profile and gamma branches.

\[ Y_{dtri}\sim\operatorname{NB2}(\mu_{dti},\alpha_{dti}), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y_{dtri})=\mu_{dti}+\alpha_{dti}\mu_{dti}^{2}, \qquad r_{dti}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{dti}}. \] \[ \ell_{\mathrm{NB2}}(y,\mu,\alpha) =\log\Gamma(r)-\log\Gamma(y+r)+\log\Gamma(y+1) -r\log r-y\log\mu+(r+y)\log(r+\mu). \]

The important point is not only the variance. Differentiate the ordinary NB2 NLL with respect to the logarithm of the mean (the natural scale for a positive multiplicative model):

\[ \frac{\partial\ell_{\mathrm{NB2}}}{\partial\log\mu} =\mu\frac{\partial\ell_{\mathrm{NB2}}}{\partial\mu} =\frac{\mu-y}{1+\alpha\mu}. \tag{1} \]

Thus, when \(\alpha\mu\) is large, the denominator is large and the mean/gamma update is attenuated. A large alpha can therefore be a cause of a missed strong bias. But the arrow can point the other way too: if gamma already misses a systematic site, the dispersion head can increase alpha to explain the remaining residual. The observed standard-run association was:

mean alpha at missed sites = 0.3808
mean alpha at correct sites = 0.1482
missed / correct ratio = 2.57

This association alone is not causal. The matched experiment below asks whether removing the attenuation mechanism actually removes the gamma misses.

4. What exactly was the beta = 1 experiment?

Three runs used the same mass-free model, five-dataset panel, 0.25-read depth, seed, transcript split, validation-ID hash, batching, reliability weights, architecture, optimizer settings, training duration, and minimum-validation-loss checkpoint selection. Only the handling of alpha and its influence on the mean gradient changed. The experiment was not a new likelihood and did not change the forward prediction formula.

4.1 Condition A — ordinary learned-alpha control

In the control, alpha is learned and the usual NB2 objective is used. In the notation below, \(\theta_\mu\) denotes the parameters that influence the mean/gamma pathway and \(\theta_\alpha\) denotes the alpha head.

\[ \mathcal L_A =\ell_{\mathrm{NB2}}(y,\mu_{\theta_\mu},\alpha_{\theta_\alpha}), \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L_A}{\partial\log\mu} =\frac{\mu-y}{1+\alpha\mu}. \]

This is experiment_mode: standard_nb with nb_mean_gradient_beta: 0.0. The alpha head still receives its normal likelihood gradient. Its context input is detached and its learning rate is reduced, as in the main implementation, but alpha remains fully trainable.

4.2 Condition B — learned alpha with a fully corrected mean gradient

The beta intervention multiplies the NB2 NLL by a factor that is detached from autograd. For a general \(0\leq\beta\leq1\), the per-position mean optimization term is:

\[ w_\beta =\operatorname{stopgrad}\!\left[(1+\alpha\mu)^\beta\right], \qquad \widetilde\ell_\mu =w_\beta\,\ell_{\mathrm{NB2}}(y,\mu,\operatorname{stopgrad}(\alpha)). \] \[ \frac{\partial\widetilde\ell_\mu}{\partial\log\mu} =w_\beta\frac{\mu-y}{1+\alpha\mu} \approx(\mu-y)(1+\alpha\mu)^{\beta-1}. \tag{2} \]

The word “detached” is essential. The factor changes the size of the mean update, but the optimizer does not differentiate through \(w_\beta\) and cannot exploit the correction by changing alpha itself. Therefore:

\[ \beta=0:\;\frac{\mu-y}{1+\alpha\mu} \quad\text{(ordinary attenuation)}, \qquad \beta=\tfrac12:\;(\mu-y)(1+\alpha\mu)^{-1/2} \quad\text{(partial correction)}, \qquad \beta=1:\;\mu-y \quad\text{(approximately no attenuation)}. \]

The matched B run is stronger than merely setting beta to one in a coupled loss: it separates the two gradient routes. It constructs a mean/gamma term and an alpha-only term:

\[ \mathcal L_{\mu} =\operatorname{stopgrad}(1+\alpha\mu)\, \ell_{\mathrm{NB2}}\!\left(y,\mu,\operatorname{stopgrad}(\alpha)\right), \] \[ \mathcal L_{\alpha} =\ell_{\mathrm{NB2}}\!\left(y,\operatorname{stopgrad}(\mu),\alpha\right), \qquad \mathcal L_B=\mathcal L_{\mu}+\mathcal L_{\alpha}. \tag{3} \]

Consequently \(\mathcal L_\mu\) updates the mean, \(L^{\mathrm{bio}}\), and gamma pathway but not the alpha head, while \(\mathcal L_\alpha\) updates alpha but not the mean pathway. For \(\beta=1\), equation (2) gives \(\partial\mathcal L_\mu/\partial\log\mu\approx\mu-y\). This tests the specific hypothesis that the factor \(1/(1+\alpha\mu)\) is suppressing gamma learning, while preserving an ordinary NB2 likelihood for alpha.

This is experiment_mode: decoupled_nb_mean_gradient with nb_mean_gradient_beta: 1.0. It does not fix alpha to one, remove alpha from the forward model, or claim that the reweighted term is a probabilistic likelihood. It is an optimization surrogate only.

4.3 Condition C — fixed-alpha synthetic oracle

The oracle removes learned dispersion as a possible confounder by using the exact value that generated the synthetic counts at every dataset, transcript, replica, and position:

\[ \alpha_{dti}=\alpha_{\mathrm{true}}=0.1, \qquad \mathcal L_C =\ell_{\mathrm{NB2}}(y,\mu,0.1). \]

This is experiment_mode: fixed_alpha. The alpha head does not participate in the forward pass or optimizer. It is a diagnostic oracle, not a proposed real-data assumption.

4.4 What was and was not compared

A Standard learned alpha. Ordinary NB2 NLL, beta = 0. Alpha remains learned with detached context input and its reduced learning rate.
B Decoupled beta = 1. Mean/gamma receives the corrected gradient; alpha receives a separate ordinary NB2 gradient.
C Oracle fixed alpha. Alpha is exactly 0.1 everywhere and the alpha head does not determine the loss.
Interpretation rule. If B improves while C does not, inspect the implementation: fixing alpha to the true generating value should remove alpha-driven attenuation. If C improves strongly but B does not, alpha is involved but the problem is not explained by attenuation alone. If neither improves, high alpha is most consistent with a consequence or correlate of the gamma failure.

Fixing alpha produces only a small absolute change of 0.034 percentage points, not the large collapse expected under an alpha-causal explanation. Moreover, 1,492 of A's 1,613 misses are also missed by C (92.5%; Jaccard 0.896), and 1,468 sites are missed in all three conditions.

Causal interpretation: elevated alpha is predominantly a consequence or correlate of a persistent gamma failure. It may amplify or describe uncertainty at some sites, but it does not explain why the main miss set exists.

Direction of the boundary effect

In the standard run, all 1,236 misses for the 3′ AA/CC/GG conditions are at the final four codons. AU-composition misses appear mainly at the end (109 end, 37 start, 3 interior), while GC-composition misses appear mainly at the start (179 start, 32 end, 17 interior). This directionality matches a fragment window crossing the corresponding CDS boundary.

A model-level failure should not normally collapse from roughly 42% near the boundary to roughly 0.01% immediately in the interior. That abrupt spatial transition points to missing or misaligned input information.

7. Direct audit of what sequence the programmed rule uses

The model currently loads MANE.selection.sequence_embeddings_with_css.parquet, whose sequence field is built from MANE CDS codons. It includes the CDS sequence but not raw 5′- and 3′-UTR flanks. The synthetic bias, however, is defined on an ideal 30-nt RPF around each P-site.

8. Why two BiGRU layers are powerful enough—but cannot recover absent bases

The current dataset-bias branch receives a codon embedding, three ordered nucleotide embeddings, an amino-acid embedding, and dataset identity at every codon. A two-layer bidirectional GRU with hidden size 256 then builds the local context used by the gamma head. Ten codon steps therefore contain 30 ordered nucleotides.

What it can learn Dinucleotide identity, AU/GC composition, upstream/downstream context, and dataset-specific rules when the required nucleotides are in the input.
What it cannot infer exactly Whether unobserved UTR bases are AA, CC, GC-rich, or AU-rich. Model depth cannot reconstruct information that was never supplied.

“Two layers” does not mean a fixed 30-nt receptive field. A single bidirectional recurrent layer already has access to the complete supplied sequence; the second layer adds transformation capacity. A learned local convolution could make 30-nt counting easier, but it would still fail when part of the window lies outside the input.

The approximately 0.01% interior miss rate is the empirical capacity test: when the complete rule is observable, the existing sequence encoder almost always recovers it. The edge discontinuity is therefore much stronger evidence for missing context than for an undersized BiGRU.

9. The principled fix: raw sequence flanks, not handcrafted bias features

The model should not receive indicators such as “3′ AA,” “AU-rich RPF,” or the programmed gamma. It should continue learning these properties from raw sequence. The necessary change is to make the raw sequence complete around every modeled P-site.

  • Add the matching MANE transcript's 5′-UTR and 3′-UTR nucleotide context.
  • Preserve CDS P-site coordinates and emit predictions only for the existing CDS profile positions.
  • Provide at least the bases needed for offsets −12 through +17; retaining 30 nt of flank on both sides is safer.
  • Use the same transcript isoform for the CDS, UTRs, synthetic annotations, and split IDs.
  • Represent genuinely unavailable bases with an explicit unknown/boundary token rather than inventing nucleotides.
  • Validate P-site/RPF alignment before training with the exact motif reconstruction audit used above.
UTR sequence is not privileged experimental information. It is ordinary transcript sequence available from annotation. The network still has to discover the dataset-specific bias rule itself.

What will not solve the missing-information problem

  • More start/stop position features: they identify an edge but not the unseen bases.
  • A deeper or wider GRU alone: it still receives the same incomplete sequence.
  • Further alpha-gradient corrections: the fixed-alpha oracle preserves the miss set.
  • Handcrafted motif or composition flags: they would leak the answer instead of testing sequence learning.

If UTR context is intentionally excluded, the scientifically consistent alternatives are to apply synthetic biases only where the full ideal RPF is contained in the CDS, or to exclude boundary-crossing sites from exact gamma recovery metrics. Exact prediction from CDS-only input should not be claimed for labels generated from outside-CDS bases.

10. The decisive next experiment

  1. Build a sequence artifact containing the same CDS plus raw flanks.
  2. Verify that the 5′ and 3′ programmed rules are reconstructible at every evaluated P-site.
  3. Keep the model, split, panel, seed, loss, sampling, and training schedule unchanged.
  4. First rerun condition C with alpha fixed at 0.1, because it removes alpha as a confounder.
  5. Then rerun condition A to verify the same result under learned alpha.

Scope and limitations

The causal numbers in this report come from the matched mass-free, five-dataset, 0.25-reads-per-codon alpha-causality group created on 25–26 August 2026. The spatial audit uses its minimum-validation-loss predictions and the common validation-ID hash 3b7d23159a16. Other depths and panels support broader gamma-recovery analyses, but they were not substituted into this matched causal comparison.

The sequence-offset check establishes label observability, not yet model recovery after UTRs are supplied. A flank-complete rerun is still required before describing the boundary problem as experimentally resolved.
